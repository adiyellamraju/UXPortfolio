Portfolio / Experimentation Infrastructure
Platform UX Experimentation AI/ML Trust Salesforce

You can't trust a decision you can't test.

Experimentation infrastructure for Salesforce's Customer 360: a way for marketers and admins to run variations of a decision, anywhere in the platform, and see for themselves whether the AI is right. Designed and shipped into the platform before the Personalization Core redesign, as its own chapter of work.

Platform-wide Personalization points and email journeys alike
2024 Shipped ahead of the Core platform redesign
Marketers + admins Statistical tooling for non-statisticians
Where this sits A standalone chapter, shipped into the platform before the Personalization Core redesign, so the visual language here predates that system. Folding experimentation into the new configuration spine was in flight when I left Salesforce; I handed it to another designer.
01

The problem

The platform got smarter. Confidence in it didn't.

Salesforce's users were asking for more visibility and transparency into how decisions get configured and how they perform. They had no reliable way to establish the credibility of the content being delivered to their own customers, a gap that only widened as AI and ML took over more of the decisioning.

The engine was making more calls, more often, on behalf of more brands. But nothing in the platform let a marketer prove a call was the right one. Personalization without verification is just a guess at scale.

How might we enhance Customer 360 with experimentation capabilities that build trust and confidence in decisioning, anywhere in the platform, and move toward the vision of 1:1 hyper-personalization?
02

Audience

Two people, two very different moments of doubt.

Marketing specialists and website admins. Neither is a statistician; both are accountable for what a customer sees. The experimentation model had to serve them at the exact point where they hesitate.

Marketing specialist · Website admin At the personalization point "When I'm configuring my personalization point, I want to experiment with multiple variations of decisions so I can trust and improve my recommendations."
Marketing specialist · Website admin At the email journey "When I'm configuring my email journey, I want to randomly select audience members to experiment with multiple journey variations of the split so I can put people on the right journey path."

Two surfaces, one underlying need: split the audience, run the variations, and show me which one won, without leaving the flow I'm already in.

03

How I got staffed

The ask came from the top.

The senior director of product went to my manager and asked for design help on experimentation, an effort important enough that it was being escalated rather than queued. I got staffed onto it.

My core partnership team was small and senior: a director of product, a senior PM, an engineering architect, and an engineering manager. No existing design work, no shared definition of what experimentation even meant inside Salesforce Personalization Services. That was the first thing to fix.

04

Unpacking the problem

Before designing anything, I ran a workshop.

The team had a mandate but not a shared picture. I ran a working session to get everyone to the same definition, the same users, and the same set of jobs, then took the output and turned it into journey maps and a competitive teardown. The question on the board was blunt: what is experimentation in a Salesforce Personalization Services context, and how do we become the single source of truth for it?

Customer problem: marketers want to make decisions with confidence. Salesforce problem: five different systems that don't all work the same way.

That second half is what made this a platform problem rather than a feature problem. Experimentation already existed in pockets across Salesforce, each with its own model and its own UI. Standardizing it meant designing a framework for C360, not a screen for one app.

Setting the table. Opening frame of the workshop: the goal on the board, plus every PRD, prior exploration, and piece of research the team had produced so nobody argued from memory.

What, Why, Who. The brief we built together. "What" landed on a standardized E13n framework for C360 apps. "Why" surfaced the real customer sentiment: is this working, should I do this, can I delegate my decision-making to ML? "Who" produced ten roles, from technical marketer to designer-developer.

Jobs to be done. I organized the jobs into four domains (Learn, Plan, Build, Run) which became the spine of everything after. This is where the hard configuration questions surfaced: how many variations, relative weight allocation, success metrics, and the three different ways a marketer might want a winner picked.

Lo-fi journey map. The first pass at sequence: create → variations → goal → allocation → winner selection → preview → seed-list test → launch → analyze. Branches under each step capture the real choices, including the two places AI could take over.

The journey, with the doubt mapped in. Same flow, now with the marketing specialist's questions and decision points laid over it. How do I decide on the variations? AI/ML or manual? should it be system-controlled? does it need to be statistically significant? Those questions are the design brief.

Competitive teardown: web. Step-by-step reconstruction of how Optimizely and Adobe Target handle targeting, variation creation, metrics, and traffic distribution. Useful less for what to copy than for where every one of them makes the marketer think like a statistician.

Competitive teardown: email. Adobe Campaign's A/B flow: number of variants, winning strategy, test duration, target breakdown. The email case has a different shape from web (the split happens once, at send) and that difference drove a lot of the framework.

What already existed inside C360. The audit that proved the point: web experimentation and flow experimentation were already shipping, built separately, behaving differently. Any standard had to absorb them rather than replace them.

05

Deconstructing the architecture

To design experimentation, I had to find where it actually lives.

Experimentation isn't a screen. It's a step inside a request. Before I could design the configuration experience, I needed to understand the decisioning pipeline well enough to know exactly where an experiment interrupts it, what it can see at that moment, and what it's allowed to change. So I sat with the engineering architect and took the pipeline apart.

The engineering architecture, interrogated. Orange notes are the plain-language questions I needed answered: who is this user, what do they qualify for, is there an experiment, is AI involved, what gets recorded. Pink notes are the things nobody could answer yet.

Working through it surfaced the constraint that shaped everything downstream: experimentation sits after qualification and before decisioning. The user is already matched to a Data Cloud profile and already qualified for a personalization point by the time the experiment picks a cohort. That ordering is why an experiment can only ever choose among decisions the marketer has already made eligible, and why the configuration UI had to be built into the decision flow rather than bolted alongside it.

Translating the architecture into seven plain sentences did more for alignment than any wireframe. Once PM, engineering, and design were pointing at the same box, "where does experimentation go?" stopped being an argument.
06

Where should it live?

The decision I argued for, and lost.

The architecture work answered when experimentation happens. It didn't settle what it attaches to, and that turned out to be the most consequential question in the project.

Personalization has two levels. A personalization point is a location: the homepage hero, a product-page slot. A decision is what to show there, to whom, driven by a recommender or manual content. A single personalization point can hold several decisions. My position was that an experiment belongs on the decision, because that is where the marketer has already declared an audience and a piece of content. Attaching it a level up means re-asking for both.

So I built the mental model and argued from it rather than from preference.

Working backwards from the marketer's goal (show the best content, to this audience, in this location) through how content is actually architected in our system, to the sentence that ends the argument: I have already defined my audience in a decision. Why should I redefine it for an experiment?

This was a classic engineering-first versus customer-first split. The system had been architected around the personalization point, and attaching experimentation there kept it platform-centric: easier to expose to other clouds, easier to offer as experimentation as a service. Those are real arguments, and they carried the day.

I lost this one. The experiment attached to the personalization point, not the decision, and that is why targeting rules and content reappear inside the wizard even though the decision already holds them. It was also a masterclass in disagree and commit: the business needed to ship, so I committed and designed the best version of a model I had argued against.

The model didn't go away, though. It went on the record, which is what made the later reversal possible.

07

The designs

Designing the best version of the model I argued against.

Attached at the personalization point, the wizard has to re-establish audience and content itself, so my job became making that re-asking as cheap as possible: inherit everything the platform already knows, ask for the fewest genuine decisions, and never let a marketer wonder what the experiment is about to do. Four steps: Name → Metrics → Targeting → Cohorts, one decision each, with a persistent explainer rail carrying the vocabulary experimentation demands and most marketers don't have yet.

The wizard shape wasn't a stylistic choice. The competitive teardowns showed Optimizely and Adobe both front-loading configuration into dense single screens, which works when the operator is a specialist. Our audience was a marketing specialist and a website admin, sometimes testing an ML recommender they didn't build. Sequence beat density.

Step 1. Name it, and show what it already knows. Inherited properties render as read-only chips instead of five more fields, and the explainer rail on the right is permanent, not a tooltip.

Step 2. Force one primary metric. Exactly one, required: the number the winner is judged on. Secondaries are optional and shown back as removable pills rather than hidden behind "3 Selected."

Step 3. The step I argued shouldn't exist. The attachment level surfaces here: the marketer re-declares an audience the decision already holds. Same Resource / Operator / Value row as everywhere else, so at least nothing new has to be learned.

Step 4. Cohorts, the hard screen. A cohort is a percentage plus a piece of content, and nothing else. Control can't be deleted, and "No Recommender" is a first-class option.

Same shell, different content type. Manual content swaps only the content region. The container, stepper, and status chips are identical. One model covers "test two algorithms" and "test two headlines."

Two cohorts: collapse, and do the math for me. Finished cohorts collapse to a summary row that doubles as the audit trail, and "Distribute Remaining" handles the allocation arithmetic instead of validating it afterwards.

Four cohorts, and an honest incomplete state. Scales without redesign. Cohort 4 is flagged Define Content right on its row, so the problem is surfaced where the fix lives rather than as a dead Next button.

The decisions behind those screens

Step 1 · Naming Inheritance made visible Rather than re-asking for data space, profile data graph, type, source, and schema, the experiment inherits them and displays them as read-only chips. It answers "what am I actually testing on?" before the question gets asked, and removes five fields from step 1.
Every step · Guidance The help rail is permanent Experimentation is a vocabulary problem as much as a configuration problem, so the definition sits beside the field rather than behind a tooltip or a separate guidance tab. That lesson carried over from the Personalization Campaign work, where tabbed help got abandoned after the first visit.
Step 2 · Metrics The constraint is the design Most tools let you pick a pile of metrics and sort out significance later, which is how a test ends with four numbers and no decision. Forcing a single primary metric makes the marketer commit to what success means before any traffic is split, which is the highest-leverage moment to ask.
Step 4 · Cohorts Two decisions, visible together The stepper handles allocation, the picker handles content, and they never drift apart. Control is structural rather than deletable, and "No Recommender" is offered outright, since the most honest test of a recommender is often against no recommender at all.
Step 4 · Scale Collapsed rows as audit trail Name, status chip, percentage. The summary row keeps the screen readable as cohorts multiply and becomes the review artifact, so a four-way split can be audited in one glance without expanding anything.
Step 4 · Errors Incomplete states point at themselves A flagged cohort row does double duty as diagnosis and link to the fix, instead of a disabled primary button that leaves the marketer guessing which of four collapsed rows is at fault.
The through-line across all seven screens: never ask for something the platform already knows, and never hide what the experiment is about to do. Inherited properties as chips, one required primary metric, live catalog values, a locked control cohort, collapsed rows that double as an audit trail, and an incomplete state that points at itself.
08

Reading the result

Setup was half the problem. The other half was the answer.

Configuration only earns trust if the result is legible. This is the performance summary: one screen that has to answer "which variation won," "by how much," and "should I believe it" for someone who does not read confidence intervals for a living.

The Shoe Sales Experiment reading out: cohort table at the top, primary metric charted over the test window, credible intervals and win probabilities per variation, secondary metrics tabbed below.

Statistics · Language "Chance to beat control," not p-values The primary judgment is stated as a probability a marketer can act on, with a credible interval beside it rather than a significance test to interpret. Same rigor underneath, phrased as a decision instead of a hypothesis.
Statistics · Honesty Two questions, both answered Chance to beat control and chance to beat all sit side by side, because a variation can win against baseline and still lose to a sibling. Showing only the first is how a four-way test gets read as a two-way one.
Hierarchy Primary metric owns the screen The metric the marketer committed to in step 2 gets the chart and the interval table. Secondary metrics are tabbed below rather than stacked, so the screen keeps making one argument instead of four.
Traceability Content named in every row Rows read "Variation 1 · Top Sellers," not "Variation 1." The cohort's content carries through from setup to result, so an outcome can always be traced back to the specific recommender that produced it.
Direction Lift with a direction, always Every lift figure carries an arrow and a color, including the ones pointing down. A test that shows the AI losing is the test that makes the wins believable.
Control Stop is a primary action Stop Experiment sits at the top of the page as the primary button while the test runs. The fastest way to make people comfortable shipping an experiment is to make ending one obvious.
Trust in AI decisioning came down to this screen. The engine can be right, but if the marketer cannot read why, nothing about their confidence changes.
09

Outcomes

An experimentation service, not an experimentation feature.

What shipped was the first experimentation service at Salesforce built to work two ways at once: inside Personalization as a native step, and as a service other clouds could call. The attachment decision I argued against is exactly what made the second half possible, which is the part of losing that argument worth saying out loud.

Scope Marketing Cloud and beyond Designed for Personalization first, but structured so experimentation could be consumed across Marketing Cloud and other clouds rather than rebuilt per surface.
Validation 10 SEs and AEs, unanimous I walked the flow with ten solution engineers and account executives. All were confident their accounts would use it, and read it as an adoption driver for personalization as a whole.
Pattern Experimentation as a service One configuration model and one result surface, callable from anywhere in the platform. The platform-centric attachment that cost the ideal UX bought the reuse.
Next Automated winner selection The roadmap from here was AI in the loop: let the engine promote a winner as confidence accrues, with the marketer setting the guardrails rather than watching the chart.
Then the reversal. During the Personalization Core redesign, the head of engineering revisited the attachment question with customer feedback, research, and the mental model in hand, and came around. Experimentation 2.0 moved to the decision level. My last work before leaving Salesforce was redesigning it that way and handing it to another designer.

Which is the actual lesson from this project. Committing to a decision I disagreed with kept the business moving, and keeping the argument on the record meant it was still there when the evidence caught up.
